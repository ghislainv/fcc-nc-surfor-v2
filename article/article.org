* Materials and Methods

** Global forest datasets

We considered two global or pantropical forest datasets to obtain maps of forest cover and forest cover change in New Caledonia: the Global Forest Change (GFC) dataset [cite:@Hansen2013] and the Tropical Moist Forest (TMF) dataset [cite:@Vancutsem2021]. Both datasets rely on the analysis of the Landsat satellite image archive at a resolution of 30 m.

GFC is a global dataset providing estimates of (i) tree cover percentage for the year 2000, (ii) forest cover loss (change from forest to non-forest) on an annual basis from 2001 to 2025 (for GFC version 1.13 used in this study), and (iii) forest cover gain (canopy closure transitions from non-forest to forest) for the period 2000--2012 only. The tree cover percentage for the year 2000 provided by GFC ranges from 0 to 100, so that GFC can be used to characterize both dry and moist tropical forests. Nonetheless, a tree cover threshold must be chosen to define the initial forest cover for the year 2000. Some authors have used threshold values from 65 to 75% to define the moist forest in the tropics [cite:@Vieilledent2018; @Aleman2017] but this threshold can be much lower for dry forest for example [cite:@Aleman2017] and is variable between regions.

Contrary to GFC, the TMF dataset does not cover the entire globe but is available at the pantropical scale for evergreen forests, which can encompass both dry and moist tropical forests or only moist non-deciduous forests depending on the region considered. In New-Caledonia, a majority of the tree species from the three forest types (mangroves, dry and moist forests) are deciduous [cite:@BGCI2021], such that the TMF dataset can be used to characterize all forest types in New-Caledonia. TMF provides an annual product from the beginning of the archive (starting from 1990 depending on the location) to 2025 (for TMF version 2025 used in this study) where each pixel is categorized in 6 classes at each date: (1) undisturbed forest, (2) degraded forest, (3) deforested land, (4) forest regrowth, (5) permanent or seasonal water, and (6) other land cover.

** Wall-to-wall forest cover and forest cover change maps for New Caledonia

We first derived a wall-to-wall 2021 forest cover map for New-Caledonia using each of the two data-sets. We chose this year because we had access to very high resolution 2021 Pléiades satellite images on a large portion of New Caledonia land area for validating the forest cover map by photo-interpretation. To obtain the 2021 forest cover map for New Caledonia based on the GFC dataset, we first considered the forest pixels for the year 2000 assuming that a pixel was forested if it had a tree cover $\geq \theta$. We tested values of $\theta$ ranging from 10 to 90 in increments of 10. Second, we identified the deforested pixels for the years 2001 to 2020 using the forest cover loss product provided by GFC. We thus obtained a forest cover map for January 1$^{\text{st}}$, 2021. We did not consider the forest gain to derive the 2021 forest cover map as it is only provided for the period 2000--2012 in the GFC dataset. We identified $\theta^{\star}$, the optimal tree cover threshold for GFC which maximized the overall accuracy when comparing the 2021 forest cover map with the results from photo-interpretation (more details in the following sections). For TMF, we considered the band "Dec2020" of the annual product which indicates the pixel state at the end of the year 2020 to obtain a forest cover map for January 1$^{\text{st}}$, 2021. We classified pixels as forested if they fell in the following categories: (1) undisturbed forest, (2) degraded forest, or (4) forest regrowth. 

Second, we derived wall-to-wall forest cover change maps for the two periods 2000--2008 and 2008--2021. These years corresponded to years for which we had access to high resolution images on a portion of New Caledonia for validating the forest cover change map by photo-interpretation. For GFC, we used the optimal tree cover threshold $\theta^{\star}$ to define the forest in 2000 and derive the forest cover change maps for the two periods. For the first period (2000--2008), forest cover loss was defined as pixels which were forest in 2000 and deforested during the years 2001 to 2007. Forest cover in 2008 combined the forest pixels in 2000 which were not deforested in 2000--2008 and pixels of forest cover gain provided by GFC for the period 2000--2012. For the second period (2008--2021), we did not considered forest cover gain which was not available after 2012 in the GFC dataset. Forest cover in 2008 was defined as forested pixels in 2000 which were not deforested in the period 2001--2007. Forest cover in 2021 was defined as forested pixels in 2000 which were not deforested in the period 2001--2020. Forest cover loss for the period 2008--2021 was obtained from the forest cover at the two dates. For TMF, we used the annual product to derive forest cover maps for the years 2000, 2008, and 2021. We classified pixels as forested if they fell in the following categories: (1) undisturbed forest, (2) degraded forest, or (4) regrowth. For the two periods 2000--2008 and 2008--2021, and the two datasets GFC and TMF (except for GFC on 2008--2021), we ended up with four categories for forest cover change: "loss", "gain", "stable forest", and "stable non-forest". For GFC on 2008--2021, for which forest gain is unavailable, we only obtained three categories: "loss", "stable forest", and "stable non-forest".

Both GFC and TMF datasets are available on Google Earth Engine (GEE), a cloud-based platform by Google that combines a catalog of satellite imagery and environmental data with computing facilities [cite:@Gorelick2017]. Maps were obtained using the =geefcc= Python package (https://pypi.org/project/geefcc/) which allows computing and downloading forest cover change maps from the GFC and TMF datasets available on GEE.

** Photo-interpretation

*** Forest-cover for the year 2021

To obtain a reference dataset for the forest cover, we used very high spatial resolution (< 10 m) Pléiades satellite images covering the Southern Province of New Caledonia for the year 2021 (Fig. 1). Spatial resolution is 0.5 m for panchromatic images (one black and white band) and 2 m for multispectral images (four bands with red, green, blue and near infra-red bands). These non-free images were provided by the technical services of the Southern Province. The Southern Province covers about 38% of New Caledonia land area of 18,576 km^{2}.

We randomly sampled 1050 points in the Southern Province that were used to define the center of a cluster of nine (three by three) 30 m pixels. We obtained a total of 9450 sampled pixels. Three interpreters identified the forest cover status of the pixels by photo-interpreting the Pléiades satellite images. They used the Collect Earth Online software (https://www.collect.earth/) to record their observations. Each interpreter classified a part (332) of the 1050 nine-pixel clusters independently from the other interpreters. Five percent of the clusters (54 clusters, corresponding to 486 pixels) were interpreted by the three interpreters in order to quantify the disagreement between interpreters [cite:@Olofsson2014].

The interpreters assigned each pixel to one of three forest cover classes: "forest", "non-forest", or "not interpretable". The class "not interpretable" was applied to pixels obscured by clouds or shadows, or where the quality of the Pléiades imagery was insufficient to make a reliable determination. A pixel was classified as "forest" if a majority of the 30 by 30 m pixel was covered by forest. Forest was defined as a contiguous area of land covered by tree crowns of height greater than or equal to 5 m, exhibiting specific tonal, textural, and spatial patterns clearly distinct from other land cover types. The main other land cover types encountered in New Caledonia include bare soil, grasslands and savanna, mining maquis (a shrubland vegetation type characteristic of nutrient-poor ultramafic soils) and urban areas.

The interpreters made no distinction between different forest types existing in New Caledonia. Although moist tropical forest is the dominant forest type in New Caledonia (>500,000 ha, [cite//b:@Myers2000]), relict dry tropical forests (10,000 ha) occur in the drier rain shadow of mountains along the west coast [cite:@Bouchet1995], while tree plantations are found in certain specific locations (e.g. 4500 ha of /Pinus caribaea/ Morelet (Pinaceae) at the Tango plateau) and mangroves fringe the coastline (estimated at 29,627 ha in 2025 after [cite//b:@Bunting2022]).

A particular challenge in New Caledonia lies in distinguishing mining maquis from forest on satellite imagery, as well-developed maquis stands can reach several meters in height, making them visually similar to low-stature forest formations, yet typically remaining below the 5 m height threshold used to define forest. Interpreters underwent a training phase prior to performing the pixel classification, during which difficult cases were discussed and clarification guidelines were established in order to minimize variability between interpreters.

*** Forest cover change for periods 2000--2008 and 2008--2021

To obtain reference datasets for the study of the forest cover change for the periods 2000--2008 and 2008--2021, we used a combination of 0.5 m resolution aerial photographs for the years 2000, 2007, and 2008, and Pléiades satellite images for the year 2021. Aerial photographs were provided by the DITTT ("/Direction des Infrastructures, de la Topographie et des Transports Terrestres/") of the Government of New Caledonia. Intersection of aerial photographs in 2000 and 2007/2008 covered an area of 109,355 ha, corresponding to 6% of New Caledonia land area. Intersection of aerial photographs in 2007/2008 and Pléiades images in 2021 covered an area of 558,077 ha, corresponding to 30% of New Caledonia land area.

Stratified sampling was employed to collect the reference datasets for forest cover change. This approach was adopted given the rarity of forest change events, which usually occur at a rate close to 1% per year [cite:@Olofsson2014]. Stratification was based on the classes of change obtained from the GFC and TMF datasets regarding the forest cover: "gain" or "loss". To assess change between 2000 and 2008, we drew a stratified sample of 1000 points: 600 in areas classified as loss by either GFC (300 points) or TMF (300 points), and 400 in areas classified as gain by either GFC (200 points) or TMF (200 points). Since GFC gain data are only available for the period 2000--2012 [cite:@Hansen2013], we used this layer to sample the 200 points corresponding to GFC gain for the period 2000--2008. To assess change between 2008 and 2021, we drew a stratified sample of 850 points: 600 in areas classified as loss by either GFC (300 points) or TMF (300 points), and 250 in areas classified as gain by TMF. As before, points were used to define the center of a cluster of nine (three by three) 30 m pixels. We thus obtained a total of 9000 sampled pixels for the period 2000--2008 and 7650 pixels for the period 2008--2021.

Two interpreters classified the 9000 and 7650 pixels for the year 2000 and 2021 respectively, while three interpreters classified the 16650 pixels for the pivotal year 2008. As previously, five percent of the pixels were classified by several interpreters at each date to be able to estimate interpreter disagreement: by two interpreters for years 2000 and 2021, and by three interpreters for the year 2008. Pixels were classified as "forest", "non-forest, or "not interpretable" at each date using the same forest definition as before. From the forest cover classification at each date, we derived five classes of forest cover change for the two periods: "loss", "gain", "stable forest", "stable non-forest", and "not interpretable".

** Disagreement between interpreters

We obtained four datasets after photo-interpretation. One dataset, based on randomly sampled points, was used to analyze forest cover in 2021. The other three datasets, generated through stratified sampling, represent forest cover in 2000, 2008, and 2021 and were used to estimate forest cover change between these dates. To assess the level of disagreement among interpreters, each sample point interpreted by at least two of the three interpreters was analyzed across all four datasets. Raw disagreement was first quantified as the proportion of sample points for which at least two interpreters assigned different land cover classes (Forest, Non-Forest, or Not Interpretable). To provide a chance-corrected measure of agreement, Krippendorff's Alpha ($\alpha_{K}$) [cite:@Krippendorff2008] was then computed for each dataset individually and across all datasets combined. The observed disagreement ($D_{o}$) was defined as the mean, over all sample points, of the proportion of discordant pairs among all possible pairs of annotations at that point. The expected disagreement by chance ($D_{e}$) was derived from the marginal class frequencies across all annotations, and equals one minus the sum of squared class frequencies (i.e., the probability that two randomly drawn annotations belong to different classes). Krippendorff's Alpha was then calculated as $\alpha_{K} = 1 - (D_{o} / D_{e})$, where a value of 1 indicates perfect agreement and 0 indicates agreement no better than chance. Finally, to identify whether a single interpreter was driving disagreement, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed: each interpreter was removed in turn, Alpha was recomputed on the remaining two interpreters across all datasets, and the change in Alpha relative to the full three-interpreter value was recorded.
